What is actually in here

Everything below is either counted off the live index when this page loads, or a link to the decision that made it so. Job search runs on claims that are hard to check; these are meant to be easy.

Where the listings come from

Companies run their hiring on an applicant tracking system — the software that owns their careers page and their application form. HeadStart reads those boards directly, one scraper per provider, and keeps what it finds. The result is the employer's own posting, not a copy of it, and the link on every card opens on the employer's own domain.

This is the whole reason the index catches roles that never reach the big aggregators: an employer has to opt in to syndicate a job outwards, and plenty never do.

What is deliberately left out

  • Anything that is not a tech role. Only software-engineering and adjacent openings are indexed. The filter that decides is tuned to keep borderline roles rather than drop them, so a little non-tech gets through — that direction is on purpose.
  • Listings not written in English. The matching model is English-only, and it would rank a German description confidently and wrongly. Those boards are still read; their jobs are held out of search until multilingual matching exists.
  • Anything an employer paid to place. There is no such mechanism. Ordering is similarity to your query and nothing else.

What the index does not know

Boards vary in what they publish, so a field being missing is usually the employer's silence rather than a bug. These are counted from the served index right now — if the pipeline gets worse at something, this number falls.

Counting…

Percentages are of listings in the index. They say nothing about boards not yet discovered or jobs never scraped — a high number here is not a claim of completeness.

How results get ordered

  • Your words are matched on meaning. The query and every job description are turned into vectors, and results come back by similarity. "Backend engineer at a climate startup" works; it is not looking for those words.
  • The match percentage is relative, not absolute. Raw similarity sits in a narrow band — a strong on-topic query tops out around 0.78, an absurd one still scores 0.66 — so the badge stretches that band to 0–100% to make results comparable to each other. A 90% means "among the closest things in this index", never "90% suitable for you".
  • Filters narrow; the query ranks. The rail on the left is exact and deterministic. The search box is the only fuzzy part, which is why years and salary belong in the filters rather than in the sentence.
  • Sorting by date sorts your best matches. Choosing "newest" reorders the strong matches by date rather than date-sorting the whole index, which would bury relevance.

How a closed job leaves

Boards are re-read on a rolling schedule. When a posting is missing from its board, it is marked unconfirmed rather than deleted; it is removed only if the next read of that same board misses it too. One failed fetch therefore cannot delete a live job, and a genuinely closed one takes two passes to go.

Which means a dead link is possible, and the honest description of the window is "hours, not minutes". Each run reads a slice of boards rather than all of them, so a quiet board is revisited less often than a busy one.

What is stored about you

  • Your email address, from Google sign-in — the key your saved work hangs off.
  • Anything you save yourself: saved searches, starred jobs, and a profile if you fill one in. Deleting them deletes them.
  • No email is ever sent unless you switch it on for a saved search, and every one of those carries an unsubscribe link.
  • Your searches are not sold, shared, or shown to employers. There is no employer-facing side of this product at all.
